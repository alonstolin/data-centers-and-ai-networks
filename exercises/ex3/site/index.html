Miswire Hunt

This is a 16-switch fabric (the whole network of switches and cables that connects the servers) with five hidden faults. Each of you gets one switch and can only see its own ports, so you have to compare notes to find the faults. With more students than switches, some of you will share a switch, so you can also compare notes with each other. Until all five are found, the fabric stays down.

  1. Join. Enter your email. You get one switch, and the same email always gets the same switch.
  2. Check and label. Look at each of your ports. Does what the switch heard match the plan? Mark each port with the label that fits.
  3. Report. Send your labels. They show up on the shared map.
  4. Answer. Two short questions about the root cause.
  5. Optional. Flag cables you think are broken, and place the parts of Firepath on the SDN planes (Part 2).

You are done after steps 1 to 4. The shared map fills in as classmates report during the week, so check back later. The fabric coming up is a result for the class, not something you have to wait for.

Read this first: the paper, in about half a page

“Building a fabric with thousands of cables invariably leads to multiple cabling errors. Moreover, correctly cabled links may be re-connected incorrectly after maintenance such as linecard replacement. Allowing traffic to use a miscabled link can lead to forwarding loops. Links that fail unidirectionally or develop high packet error rates should also be avoided and scheduled for replacement. To address these issues, we developed Neighbor Discovery (ND), an online liveness and peer correctness checking protocol.

Neighbor Discovery (ND) uses the configured view of cluster topology together with a switch’s local ID to determine the expected peer IDs of its local ports. It regularly exchanges its local port ID, expected peer port ID, discovered peer port ID, and link error signal. Doing so allows ND on both ends of a link to verify correct cabling.

The Interface Manager (IFM) module on each switch’s embedded stack continuously monitors the ND state of each port, declaring a port up to the routing process only if it is both PHY UP and ND UP.”

A. Singh et al., “Jupiter Rising: A Decade of Clos Topologies and Centralized Control in Google’s Datacenter Network”, SIGCOMM ’15, Section 5.2.1 (“Neighbor Discovery to Verify Connectivity”), p. 192.
Why this matters in Jupiter

With thousands of cables, some will always be plugged in wrong. Google's answer was Neighbor Discovery (ND). Every switch gets the same plan, so it knows which port should be at the far end of each cable. ND checks who is really there and tells the peer what it found. A port is only used for traffic when it is both PHY up and ND up. (Section 5.2.1, p. 192)

A problem is confirmed when switches on both sides of it have labeled their ports correctly. When all five are confirmed, the fabric comes up.

Which label?

  • OK The peer is the one in the plan, it hears you, and there are no errors.
  • Miswired The peer is a different port than the plan says.
  • One-way You hear the peer, but peer hears you says no.
  • High errors The peer is right, but the error rate is high.
  • Link down PHY is DOWN. There is no signal on the cable.
  • Peer silent PHY is up, but you hear nothing from the peer.

PHY is the physical link. ND (Neighbor Discovery) is how a switch finds out who is at the other end of a cable. Some faults cannot be seen from one switch, so also look at the shared map.

Where does the control live?

Unlocks after you send your report. It does not depend on your classmates.

Software-defined networking (SDN) moves forwarding decisions out of the individual switches and into software that sees the whole network. It uses three terms, the planes:

  • Data plane. Forwards each packet by looking it up in a table in the switch hardware.
  • Control plane. Decides what goes into those tables, for example by computing shortest paths.
  • Management plane. Configures, monitors and upgrades the devices.

Google built its own control plane, Firepath, long before “SDN” was a common term. Place each of its parts on a plane. You get an answer right away, and there is nothing to submit.

    Look at the split. The master holds the topology for the whole fabric, but each client computes its own forwarding table. In a textbook OpenFlow design the controller would do both and install ready-made entries. So Firepath is centralized in what it knows and distributed in what it computes. (Section 5.2.2)
